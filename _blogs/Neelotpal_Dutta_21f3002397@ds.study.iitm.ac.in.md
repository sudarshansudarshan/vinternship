## When Learning Stops Feeling Like Learning: A Reflection on Friction, Expectations, and Reality

When I first began this internship, I approached it with a certain kind of curiosity and openness. The structure was different from what I was used to, self-paced, hands-on, and built around figuring things out rather than being explicitly taught. That, in itself, was appealing.

In my earlier reflections, I wrote about moving quickly through the MERN stack and appreciating the autonomy the program offered. At that point, I genuinely believed that once the foundation was set, the deeper parts of the journey would build meaningfully on it.

Somewhere along the way, that expectation started to shift.

### The Turning Point

The early case studies felt reasonable. They were challenging, but in a way that pushed me to think. There was a sense of direction, even when things weren’t obvious, they felt solvable.

However, as I progressed into the later case studies, the experience became less about problem-solving and more about navigating friction that didn’t feel intentional.

A recurring issue was inconsistency. Some case studies relied on tools or libraries that hadn’t been introduced beforehand. Others assumed familiarity with specific versions of technologies without ever specifying them. In a stack like MERN, where version mismatches can fundamentally change behavior, this led to long stretches of debugging that didn’t feel educational, just confusing.

In some cases, the tools themselves added to the problem. A few case studies depended on libraries that are effectively zombieware, lacking active support or reliable documentation. One example was TypeDI. When something breaks in such tools, it becomes difficult to even know whether the issue is in your understanding or in the ecosystem itself.

Testing was another point of friction. Jest was introduced into a setup that heavily relied on TypeScript, which was the first major foundation we were asked to build on. In practice, getting Jest to work cleanly with TypeScript often turned into a struggle of configuration rather than learning. Instead of reinforcing concepts, it created additional layers of confusion.

At times, even the reference code created more questions than clarity. There were instances where code appeared to mix patterns from different versions or didn’t work as expected out of the box. Instead of guiding the learning process, this often broke it.

Struggling is an important part of learning. But there is a difference between productive struggle and avoidable ambiguity. This often felt like the latter.

### When Effort and Engagement Diverge

Another thing I found myself thinking about was engagement.

Throughout the program, there were frequent discussions about how immersive or even “addictive” the learning experience was. But looking at outcomes, a large number of participants did not complete even the initial video modules within the expected timeframe.

The timeline itself was not particularly short. The videos alone were meant to be completed in about a month, and there was additional leeway of roughly two more weeks for people to catch up. Even with that, many participants did not finish.

That contrast stayed with me.

If something is truly engaging at scale, you expect that to reflect in participation. When it doesn’t, it raises questions, not about individuals, but about whether the learning experience itself has barriers that make sustained engagement difficult.

For me, the difficulty wasn’t the volume of work. It was the nature of the friction.

### The Role of Structure

We also had regular standups multiple times a week. In theory, these are meant to create alignment and shared learning.

In practice, I often found them repetitive.

Many discussions revolved around general experiences of the program, and while it was interesting to hear different perspectives initially, over time it started to feel less meaningful. The conversations rarely translated into actionable insights or improvements in how I approached the work.

That said, there was one exception that stood out, the session around AI literacy. That discussion felt genuinely engaging. It opened up broader ideas, invited curiosity, and moved beyond the immediate boundaries of the internship tasks.

It made me realize that what I found valuable was not just structure or frequency of interaction, but the quality and depth of the conversation itself.

### What I’m Taking Away

Despite my criticisms, this experience wasn’t without value.

If anything, it made me more aware of how I learn.

I realized that clarity in learning design matters more than intensity. That good challenges guide you toward insight, while poorly framed ones push you into loops of confusion. And that engagement isn’t something that can be declared, it has to be felt and reflected in how people actually participate.

Most importantly, it changed how I evaluate learning environments. I’ve become more attentive to whether a system supports understanding, or simply assumes it.

### Closing Thoughts

This internship set out to be a journey, and in many ways, it was. Not always in the ways I expected, and not always in ways I enjoyed, but still a journey that left me with clearer opinions, sharper expectations, and a better sense of what meaningful learning looks like for me.

And sometimes, that clarity is the most valuable outcome of all.
