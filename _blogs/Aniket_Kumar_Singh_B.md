# The Code I Didn't Write: How a Chatbot Led Me to This Internship

It started with a website I didn't really build.

A few months ago, I was exploring AI tools — Claude, Vibe Coding, the usual rabbit holes — and I decided to prompt my way into a project. I typed in what I wanted, and within seconds, a complete website appeared. It was clean, functional, and impressive. I felt, for a moment, like I had made something.

But as I looked at the code, I realized I understood almost none of it. TypeScript, JavaScript patterns, component structures — the AI had used the right tools, but I had no idea why. I could generate the output. I just couldn't read the room.

That moment quietly unsettled me. Not because the AI did something wrong — it did exactly what I asked. But because I had been mistaking *output* for *understanding*. I was producing things I couldn't explain, and I started wondering what that said about what I actually knew.

In February, scrolling through LinkedIn, I came across a post from Prof. Sudarshan Iyengar about the Vinternship with VLED Lab — an internship for IITM BS students built around actually learning MERN. Not watching someone build it. Not prompting an AI to do it. Learning it, the uncomfortable way.

I enrolled because I wanted to understand the code I had been outsourcing.

---

The MERN course on ViBe was not like anything I expected. There were no reminders nudging me forward, no countdown timers making urgency feel artificial. The platform placed the material in front of me and then, essentially, stepped back. The responsibility to show up — and keep showing up — was entirely mine.

This turned out to be harder than any assignment.

There were days when I made real progress and days when I opened my laptop, stared at a concept, and closed it again without writing a single line. I had thought discipline was something you either had or didn't. I was learning it was something you built, slowly, through small decisions no one was watching you make.

What changed my relationship with the material was realising that confusion wasn't a sign I was falling behind. It was a sign something new was trying to take shape. The moments I wanted to quit were often the moments just before something clicked.

---

Case studies shifted the ground beneath me again.

These weren't exercises with neat answers at the back of a book. They were problems that asked me to reason — to sit with ambiguity and find a way through it. For someone who had gotten comfortable generating solutions with a prompt, this was humbling. The AI wouldn't help here. The thinking had to be mine.

I remember working through a case study and feeling genuinely stuck for longer than I was comfortable with. But instead of looking for a shortcut, I stayed with it. Something about this internship had quietly trained me to trust the discomfort rather than escape it.

---

Looking back, what I carried out of this Vinternship wasn't a set of technologies. It was a different relationship with not-knowing.

I came in having learned to produce things I didn't understand. I leave having learned to sit with confusion long enough for understanding to arrive. That's a shift that will outlast every framework I was taught.

The website I built with a prompt still exists somewhere. But now I can read the code.
